Paired medication-check practice

Check on your own. Compare together.

You and a partner are nursing students on a simulated adult ward at the 08:00 medicine round. For six patients, compare the order with what was prepared, decide what happens next, then see what each of you found.

What you'll practise

  1. Checking the total amount: units, strength and quantity.
  2. Using the records and the patient's words — the administration record and the allergy box — even when the order and the medicine match.
  3. Choosing a next step with evidence, and telling agreement apart from being right.

Morning. Six patients, six medicines. Check each one on your own — don't tell your partner what you find until you've both signed. Mistakes here are how we learn; nothing you choose reaches a real patient.

On real wards, independent double checks are kept for selected high-risk medicines and tasks. In this practice you check every medicine twice so you can practise independent judgement. Source: ISMP

Before you start

  • Units. 1 g = 1000 mg. 1 mg = 1000 micrograms. Write “micrograms” and “units” in full: “µg” and “U” are easily misread. Convert to the same unit before you compare.
  • Total amount. Tablets and capsules: strength × number. Liquids: strength (mg per mL) × volume (mL). Example: 2 mg/mL × 3 mL = 6 mg.
  • Routes and forms. Oral: swallowed. Inhaled: from an inhaler, often through a spacer. Nebulised: a liquid turned into mist through a mask. IV: into a vein.
  • PRN orders. “When required”. The order says the shortest gap between doses and may give a 24-hour maximum. Use the interval written on each order.
  • Reading the chart. Allergy box at the top; the order; the administration record with date and time. Count across midnight using the date.
  • Patient identifiers. Full name, date of birth and MRN. Ask the patient to tell you, rather than asking “Are you…?”.
  • This practice. First compare the order with what was prepared on five checks. Then choose a next step and the evidence that decided it.

Finding

Everything on the tray matches the order, and it still isn't time for another dose.

Evidence

Administration record: 2.5 mg at 07:30 today. Order: 4-hourly PRN. Ward time: 08:15. The earliest next dose is 11:30.

Why it matters

Matching records don't tell you when the last dose was given; only the administration record does. Arthur told you, and the record confirmed it.

Next step

Don't give it now. Dispose of the drawn-up dose following your hospital's Schedule 8 procedure, with a witness, and record it. Tell the nurse in charge, reassess Arthur's pain and involve the prescriber if it isn't controlled.

What it is
A strong opioid pain medicine. In Australia it is a Schedule 8 controlled drug.
What to watch
Read the strength on the bottle before working out the volume: oral morphine liquid comes in more than one strength. Check the time since the last dose and any 24-hour maximum on the chart. Too much can make breathing slow and shallow.

Scope and limitations

Second Pair of Eyes is a small-group educational simulation for practising medication-record comparison, evidence-based discussion and clarification. All patient scenarios are fictional. It does not provide prescribing advice, authorise medication administration, replace local policies or professional supervision, or certify clinical competence. A correct response means agreement with the reference answer for this scenario, not proof that a real-world medication decision is safe.

Content review

Each case displays its content version, references and review status. “Sources checked” means the stated content has been checked against the listed sources; it does not mean the case has received independent professional review.
